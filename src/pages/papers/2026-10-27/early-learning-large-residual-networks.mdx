## 30-second takeaway

Does an infinitely large residual network necessarily learn rich nonlinear features? Narovlansky and Hanin give a useful **counterexample for early training**. They calculate the output and hidden-state response after the first few gradient-descent steps in a simplified nonlinear ResNet. The sequential limits are unusual but central: **width and depth first; then training-set size; finally input dimension**. Their diagrammatic expansion reveals that higher-degree learned functions of the input are increasingly suppressed at high input dimension. A carefully centered activation and output scaling proportional to the square root of input dimension can still learn a **linear** target efficiently. This is **not a theorem that all practical ResNets remain linear indefinitely**.

## Big Picture: an order of limits can change your intuition

The familiar **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)** picture describes the evolution of a function when trained weights effectively realize a fixed kernel. Mean-field models permit nontrivial representation changes. One might therefore expect a very large, very deep *nonlinear* ResNet to freely learn nonlinear functions.

**This paper:** in its specific early-time and large-input-dimension limit, nonlinear **target dependence** and nonlinear **input dependence** appear at different orders. The output can show nonlinear dependence on training labels after several updates while remaining approximately linear as a function of the test input. These statements are related but are **not equivalent**.

## Mechanism: what actually vanishes?

Write the simplified residual recurrence, where every hidden residual block has **zero additional hidden layers**:

```text
z_0(x) = W_0 x / sqrt(N0)

z_l(x) = z_(l-1)(x)
         + W_l phi(z_(l-1)(x)) / (L sqrt(N))

f(x)   = (gamma / N) W_out phi(z_L(x))
```

The weights are initially Gaussian; squared-error loss is optimized by **full-batch gradient descent**. The paper chooses layer-specific mean-field learning-rate normalization before further analyzing dependence on sample size and input dimension.

A central expansion variable is the normalized **Gram matrix** of inputs:

```text
chi_(a,b) = (x_a · x_b) / N0
```

Expanding the nonlinearity in **Hermite polynomials** and averaging Gaussian training inputs converts diagrams into powers of `1/N0`. Higher-degree learned monomials of the test input require extra Gram contractions and are more suppressed. For illustration, a target may contain

```text
y(x) = v1 · x + (v2 · x)^2 + ...
```

The authors explicitly analyze first through third training updates and organize their terms in these overlaps; extending the diagrammatic description to arbitrary updates is partly conjectural/heuristic. Under the specified scaling, **learning the linear component dominates the quadratic component** in high dimension.

### Why activation centering matters

A sufficient Gaussian-centering condition is

```text
E_(z ~ Normal(0,1)) [phi(z)] = 0.
```

It removes the zeroth Hermite coefficient. A concrete example of a centered ReLU is

```text
phi(z) = max(z, 0) - 1 / sqrt(2 pi).
```

For this special setting the authors study `gamma = sqrt(N0)` with `delta = 1`, allowing an order-one response to a linear target without the same divergence seen with more aggressive scaling. The uncentered activation or a frozen output layer can change the details. **The prescription is not a generally validated optimizer rule for arbitrary production ResNets.**

### Two axes of nonlinearity

```text
Does f depend nonlinearly on training labels y?
    -- can occur from third gradient step

Does f(x) respond nonlinearly to input x?
    -- higher monomials suppressed as N0 grows
```

The distinction helps avoid a common misreading: **departure from fixed-NTK dynamics alone does not guarantee efficient recovery of higher-degree input features**.

1. **Figure 1 — input nonlinearity test.** The measure `|f(2x) + f(0) - 2f(x)|` is zero for every affine function. Inspect how it changes with `N0`, including the alternative normalizations and the distinction between held-out and training inputs. The authors treat a near-inverse scaling as **suggestive**, not an exact universal exponent.
2. **Figure 2 — dependence on labels.** The experiment probes additivity in target-label directions. It is **not the same as** the affine-in-input measure in Fig. 1. A symmetry of the initial final-layer weights explains cancellations at two steps in the analyzed setting.
3. **Figure 3 — output scaling.** Training-error curves compare `gamma = 1, sqrt(N0), N0` across input dimensions and activation choices. Beware that extremely large gamma can destabilize the selected parameterization; it is not a direct test of generalization.
4. **Figure 4 — activation dependence.** Compare the centered activations with shifted/uncentered functions. The effect of zeroth Hermite mode on growth or divergence is a major qualification to the scaling law.

Section 7 further presents **Figures 5–10** for one-hidden-layer blocks or a frozen readout. They support some trends but show that finer symmetry-based claims depend on architecture.

## Authors report, AI interpretation, and caveats

### Authors report

The paper derives early-time updates (through three steps) with a diagrammatic expansion of large-width, large-depth mean-field residual-network dynamics and input inner-product statistics. Under the stated sequential high-dimensional limit, nonlinear terms in the learned input function are progressively suppressed. The authors present numerical experiments for target functions and activation/output scaling choices consistent with the theory.

### AI interpretation

This is a lesson in **the limits of simplified learning models**: even a nonlinear architecture with evolving features can show early-time high-dimensional rigidity. If a proposed infinite-width/depth mean-field model predicts easy nonlinear feature learning, its **input dimension, sample-size scaling, activation mean, time horizon, and readout trainability** must be checked before transferring that prediction to actual networks.

One productive research question is what change in joint limit (for example, training horizon or dataset scaling) is necessary for stable nonlinear features, rather than assuming that deeper or wider models resolve the issue.

### Caveats

- The core analysis assumes **special residual blocks, Gaussian initialization and inputs, full-batch squared-error training, and a definite order of infinite limits**.
- Only the first few discrete gradient steps are explicitly calculated; general-time behavior is not fully proven by the diagrammatic evidence.
- **Nonlinearity of the function in inputs** and nonlinearity of training dynamics/labels must be distinguished.
- Figure 1's apparent approximate scaling power is explicitly qualified by the authors; they note finite data and the restricted time regime.
- The paper includes extensions by experiments, but does **not** prove identical detailed results for all ResNets, Transformers, realistic non-Gaussian data, or long-time SGD.
- The arXiv v1 text, equations and figure captions were checked; calculations and simulations were **not independently reproduced**.
